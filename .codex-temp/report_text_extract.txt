1.0 Introduction
1.1 Project Background
Food waste is a huge global issue that affects homes worldwide. It is frequently caused by poor planning, overbuying, and a lack of awareness of food expiration dates. Many consumers buy groceries with good intentions, but then keep them unused in refrigerators or cabinets until they deteriorate, resulting in financial losses and unnecessary waste. Globally, families account for over 60% of food waste, making them the most significant contributors to the problem (United Nations Environment Programme, 2024). Food waste accounts for around 8-10% of global greenhouse gas emissions (Food and Agriculture Organization, 2022; United Nations Environment Programme, 2024).
As modern lifestyles become more hectic, many people are unable to carefully manage their food inventory, plan meals efficiently, or watch expiration dates. This condition raises the possibility of lost ingredients and unnecessary spoiling. As a result, there is a growing demand for a simple and dependable solution that allows homes to monitor their food consumption and eliminate unnecessary waste.
Although there are numerous recipe apps and grocery planning tools available, the majority of them work independently and do not address the underlying causes of home food waste. Many recipe apps recommend meals without taking into account the things that the user already has in the kitchen. Other inventory apps require manual tracking but do not provide intelligent expiration reminders or targeted recommendations. As a result, users continue to struggle with efficient meal planning, resulting in increased food waste. This demonstrates a significant gap in current solutions, which rarely combine ingredient tracking, expiry control, and intelligent meal recommendations into a single platform. A smart system that can learn user behavior and provide preemptive advice, has the potential to greatly enhance home food management.
As a result, the goal of this project is to create an AI-powered kitchen helper that will help people reduce food waste at home. The planned smartphone application will recommend meals based on components that are currently on hand, track expiration dates, and alert users before food spoils. The system will also include a smart grocery planner that learns from user purchase habits to anticipate future requirements and recommend new cuisines. Along with the mobile application, a promotional website will be created to highlight the system's advantages, raise awareness of food sustainability, and enable easy access to download the app. This project aims to ease meal planning, reduce family expenses, and promote more ecologically sustainable behavior in daily life by incorporating artificial intelligence into common culinary processes.
1.2 Problem statement
Household food waste is a major global issue, mostly caused by poor meal planning, over-purchasing and a lack of understanding of existing food inventory and expiration dates. Many households unintentionally reject still edible food because they lack reliable tools for tracking which ingredients they have and when they will expire. According to the United Nations Environment Programme (2024), households account for over 60% of total worldwide food waste, highlighting the critical need for consumer-level intervention.
Beyond the financial losses suffered by households, food waste has major environmental effects. Discarding food wastes the resources utilized in its production, such as water, energy and land, and decaying food adds considerably to greenhouse gas emissions (Food and Agriculture Organization, 2022). Despite the availability of recipe apps and grocery management systems, most existing solutions work in silos and do not provide an integrated strategy that includes real-time ingredient tracking, expiry monitoring and tailored meal recommendations.
Furthermore, many current systems rely primarily on manual data entry and provide generic recommendations that do not account for users' consumption or purchasing behaviors. This decreases user interest and limits the usefulness of such applications in reducing home food waste. As modern lifestyles become more fast-paced, there is a growing desire for an intelligent, user-friendly solution that can help households track, manage, and optimize their food consumption.
As a result, there is a clear academic and practical need for an AI-powered kitchen assistant that can integrate smart inventory management, predictive expiry alarms, and personalized meal planning to successfully decrease unnecessary home food waste and promote sustainable consumption habits.
1.3 Objectives
To explore and identify effective methods of reducing household food waste through ingredient tracking, recipe suggestions and AI–based reminders.
To design a mobile application and web platform that incorporate these food-waste reduction features, including a chatbot, expiry tracking system and predictive grocery planning.
To develop and implement the mobile app and website, integrating AI functionalities that suggest recipes, notify users of upcoming expiry dates and recommend future grocery purchases.
1.4 Project Scope
This project aims to create an AI-powered kitchen assistant system with two major components: a mobile application for end users and a web platform for information, promotion and app distribution. The scope defines the features, functions and boundaries of what the system will and will not include.
User Scope
This approach is primarily used by household members, particularly those who cook or handle food at home on a regular basis. This includes students, working adults, families, and anyone looking for a better systematic approach to managing food inventory.
Ingredient Tracking:Users will be able to manually input food items they purchase, including quantity, category, and expiry date. The app will also allow optional barcode scanning for easier item registration (if implemented in later stages).
Expiry Date Monitoring:Users will receive reminders when food items are close to expiration. Notifications will be pushed directly to their mobile phones to encourage timely usage.
Recipe Recommendations:The system will suggest recipes based on ingredients that users already have in their kitchen. These recommendations will consider ingredient availability, expiry urgency, and user preferences.
AI Chatbot Interaction:Users can communicate with a built-in chatbot to ask questions like “What can I cook with chicken?” or “What ingredients are expiring soon?”. This feature makes the app more interactive and user-friendly.
Predictive Grocery Planning:Users will receive suggestions on what items they may need to buy soon based on their purchase history, usage patterns, and frequently cooked meals.
User Account & Personalization:Each user will have a personal account that stores their cooking preferences, dietary restrictions, and ingredient habits, allowing the system to generate more accurate recommendations. The mobile app is the primary interactive tool for consumers to monitor, plan and manage their kitchen inventory.
System Scope
The system scope describes what the application platform will support behind the scenes, such as AI capabilities, database activities, and system interactions.
Database Management:A structured database will store user profiles, ingredient lists, expiry dates, recipe information, and purchase history. The system will retrieve and update this data dynamically as users interact with the app.
AI Processing & Recommendation Engine:The system incorporates machine learning models to analyze user behavior, predict ingredient usage, and generate recipe or grocery suggestions. The engine will consider factors such as:
Ingredient availability
Expiry urgency
User cooking patterns
Preference history
Natural Language Processing (NLP) Module:The chatbot functionality relies on NLP techniques to understand user queries, extract keywords, and generate appropriate responses. This includes simple intent detection, recommendation retrieval, and general assistance.
Notification & Reminder System:The system will automatically push alerts to users when items are nearing expiration, running low, or commonly used in upcoming meals.
Web Platform Implementation: The website will provide:
A full overview of the app’s key features
Information about food sustainability and waste reduction
A download link or QR-code to access the mobile app
Contact or support information for users
The web platform serves as a supporting system to educate and guide users but does not handle active ingredient tracking or AI processing.
Security & Authentication: The system will include secure login features, data encryption, and privacy protection mechanisms to safeguard user information.
System Limitations (Out of Scope): To maintain a realistic and achievable scope, the system will not include:
Automated image recognition for ingredient detection
Real-time grocery delivery or online store integration
Advanced nutritional analysis or diet planning
Multi-household synchronization features
These features may be explored in later project phases, but they are not part of the present development plan.

2.2 Explanation of Key terms, Terminologies and Theories
This research draws on core concepts from artificial intelligence, natural language processing, and recommendation systems. Understanding these words is critical since they create the foundation for how the system will be designed, implemented, and assessed. Artificial intelligence (AI) refers to computer systems capable of performing tasks that would normally require human intelligence, such as learning, problem solving, and pattern recognition. AI allows software to make decisions and generate ideas based on data rather than predetermined instructions. In the context of this project, AI will assist the application in analyzing user behavior, tracking ingredients, and predicting grocery needs without requiring the user to manually plan every action—particularly important given that households account for approximately 60% of global food waste, or 631 million tonnes per year (Statista, 2025a, 2025b).
A closely related idea is machine learning (ML), a discipline of artificial intelligence that focuses on developing methods that allow systems to learn from data automatically. Instead of being designed with rigid logic, machine learning systems can improve their performance by spotting patterns. In this project, ML approaches can be used to analyze user purchase history, identify common culinary tastes, and provide predictive recommendations about what goods a user might need to buy next, similar to models that predict supermarket sales using regression (Kaur & Sharma, 2023). Depending on the type and volume of data available, models such as decision trees, collaborative filtering, and simple regression-based predictions may be used.
Natural language processing (NLP) is another major subject involved in this project. NLP enables computers to interpret, understand, and generate human language. It's widely used in chatbots, voice assistants, and automated message systems. The mobile app's chatbot can speak organically with users by recognizing phrases like "What should I cook today?" or "Show me recipes using chicken and potatoes," as proven by NLP-powered recipe recommenders (Kulwinder, 2023). NLP techniques like intent detection, keyword extraction, and text classification will enable the system to respond intelligently and make relevant recommendations based on user input.
Theoretical foundations for this research include recommendation system theory, which explains how systems might suggest suitable products or actions to users. Typically, recommendation systems are classified into three types: content-based filtering, collaborative filtering, and hybrid techniques. Content-based filtering proposes products that are comparable to those the user has already used, whereas collaborative filtering recommends items based on similar users' behavior. A hybrid approach combines both methods to provide more accurate and flexible suggestions, with studies demonstrating that KNN-SVD hybrids improve food app accuracy by combining user data and nutrition attributes (Yap et al., 2024); for this project, this is appropriate for expiry-aware recipe suggestions filtered by dietary needs (Xu et al., 2024).
In addition to AI ideas, the project incorporates inventory management theory, which focuses on how resources might be recorded, arranged, and utilized efficiently. This idea applies to household settings by monitoring food products, managing expiry dates, eliminating unnecessary purchases, and preventing waste—AI solutions such as speech-based trackers have shown up to 30% waste reduction in pilots (Donnelly, 2023). Implementing inventory management concepts helps users understand what ingredients they already have and what needs to be used soon. This hypothesis supports the project's main purpose, which is to reduce food waste through increased awareness and planning.
Finally, user-centered design (UCD) concepts will be used to guide the system's entire development. UCD prioritizes user needs, behavior, goals, and comfort throughout the development process. This includes creating user-friendly interfaces, providing clear instructions, and making sure that the most important features—such as tracking expiry dates or viewing recipe suggestions—are easily accessible, with persuasive elements proven to increase engagement in waste apps (Nkwo et al., 2021; Mathisen et al., 2022). Using user-centered design makes the system more intuitive and encourages regular use, which is required to meet the project's long-term sustainability goals.
This project builds a solid theoretical foundation by integrating artificial intelligence, machine learning, natural language processing, recommendation systems, inventory management theory, and user-centered design concepts. These concepts drive the system's ability to learn from user data, communicate organically with users, efficiently organize food products, and make smart recommendations that reduce waste and promote environmentally responsible habits.
2.3 Existing Solutions and Technologies
Existing tools and studies in this area rely on a range of underlying technologies, from simple rule-based reminders to advanced hybrid recommendation models and AI-driven inventory forecasting. 2.3.1 Review of Current Systems
Many food and recipe applications are built on relatively simple technical foundations such as keyword search, tag-based filtering, and basic rules. For example, early recipe recommenders typically used string matching on ingredient names and categories, offering results if the ingredient list contained certain keywords or tags (Abbas et al., 2022). These approaches are easy to implement and fast to run, but they do not genuinely learn from user behaviour or adapt to patterns over time. In inventory-style apps, expiry reminders are often implemented as fixed date-based notifications linked to manually entered items, without any learning or prediction.
More advanced systems in research and industry use recommender system technologies to provide personalised suggestions. Collaborative filtering (user–user or item–item) and content-based filtering are the most common approaches. Collaborative filtering uses past interactions—such as ratings, favourites, or recipe choices—to identify similar users or items and recommend what similar users liked; content-based methods rely on item features like ingredients, cuisine type, or nutritional attributes to compute similarity (Abbas et al., 2022). Some commercial platforms and academic prototypes use a hybrid of both: combining user preference data with rich item features so that the model can still make reasonable suggestions even when user history is sparse (Yap et al., 2024).
In parallel, machine learning for forecasting and inventory management has become a central technology in food-related systems. Supervised learning models, including linear regression, decision trees, random forests, and gradient boosting methods such as XGBoost or LightGBM, are widely used to forecast food demand, plan stock levels, and reduce over-ordering (IBM, 2024). These models learn from historical transaction data, seasonality, day-of-week patterns, and other contextual variables to predict future consumption. In commercial kitchens, AI-based inventory and waste monitoring systems like Winnow and similar platforms combine sensor data (weights, timestamps) and computer vision to classify discarded food and generate analytics that inform procurement and menu planning (Djekic et al., 2025).
Finally, natural language processing (NLP) is increasingly used to support more natural user interaction. Many current apps still rely on simple search boxes and predefined filters, but research systems and some emerging products employ intent classification and entity recognition models to understand queries such as “show me quick vegetarian dinners with broccoli” (Abbas et al., 2022). These NLP components range from traditional models (e.g., logistic regression or support vector machines on bag-of-words features) to modern transformer-based encoders that capture context and phrasing more effectively.

2.3.3 Gaps in Existing Solutions
Despite significant progress, the current technological landscape still shows important gaps when viewed against the requirements of your project. Most existing recommendation technologies are not explicitly expiry-aware: they may recommend recipes based on ingredients or preferences, but they do not systematically prioritise items nearing their expiry date. This means that, even when users rely on AI for meal suggestions, food can still be forgotten and wasted. Similarly, many forecasting models are designed for large-scale retail or hospitality contexts and are not tailored to individual household patterns, where data is sparser and more irregular
On the implementation side, many current apps rely on cloud-only architectures and high manual input, often requiring users to repeatedly enter or update their inventory, which leads to outdated data and disengagement over time (Kwasny et al., 2025). NLP capabilities, when present, are usually limited to simple search or command phrases rather than fully conversational interactions that can handle natural, everyday questions from users. As a result, the underlying technologies have not been combined into a cohesive, user-centred system that seamlessly links inventory tracking, expiry management, hybrid recommendations, and predictive grocery planning.
The proposed AI kitchen assistant directly addresses these gaps by selecting and integrating technologies that have so far mostly been used separately. It aims to use hybrid recommendation algorithms that consider both preferences and expiry dates, supervised forecasting models adapted to household-level data, and a hybrid edge–cloud architecture that supports responsive and privacy-aware interaction. In doing so, it builds on the strengths of existing technologies but aligns them more closely with the practical requirements of home food waste reduction.
2.4 Relevant Algorithms and Methodologies
Similar challenges to this project, such as recipe suggestion, food demand forecasting, and intelligent inventory management, have been solved utilizing a variety of machine learning, recommendation, and natural language processing techniques. These algorithms serve as the methodological underpinning for services such as personalized meal recommendations, expiry-aware planning, and predictive grocery lists. The most applicable approaches are collaborative and content-based filtering, hybrid recommendation models, supervised learning for demand and purchase prediction, and natural language processing (NLP) models for conversational interfaces.
2.4.1 Recommendation Algorithms
Collaborative filtering is a popular strategy in recommender systems that anticipates user preferences based on patterns in past interactions, such as ratings or selections from comparable persons or goods (Abbas et al., 2022). In user-based or item-based k-nearest neighbours (KNN) collaborative filtering, the system finds users with similar tastes or recipes with similar profiles and then recommends items that those neighbors liked. KNN is simple and interpretable, but it can struggle with sparse data and cold-start users (Yap et al., 2024). Matrix factorization techniques, such as singular value decomposition (SVD), decompose the user-item interaction matrix into latent factors, capturing hidden dimensions of preference (for example, a user's general preference for spicy or vegetarian dishes) and typically achieve lower prediction error than pure KNN in food recommendation contexts.
Content-based filtering uses item qualities (e.g., ingredients, cuisine, cooking time, nutritional profile) rather than other users' behavior to offer recipes similar to ones a user has previously enjoyed (Abbas et al., 2022). In the food domain, content-based models frequently use vector representations of ingredients and recipe metadata to calculate similarity scores between potential recipes and the user's profile. Pure collaborative or content-based techniques have limitations—collaborative systems require a large number of users and interactions, whereas content-based systems might become too restricted and struggle to include variety (Xu et al., 2024).
To overcome these constraints, hybrid recommendation algorithms combine collaborative and content-based approaches. Yap et al. (2024) demonstrate that a hybrid model combining KNN and SVD achieves more accuracy (lower root mean square error and mean absolute error) in a meal recommender than either method alone, while also incorporating nutritional limitations. Similarly, Xu et al. (2024) present a hybrid meal recommendation framework that filters dishes based on health and nutritional constraints before ranking them using preference learning, resulting in improved personalization for chronic illness patients. Hybrid recommenders are ideal for this project because they can suggest meals that take into account user preferences, ingredient availability, and expiry dates all at once.
2.4.2 Supervised Learning for Forecasting and Inventory
Supervised learning algorithms are often used to estimate food demand and optimize inventory levels, which are closely related to anticipating what goods a user will require. Gradient boosting algorithms, such as XGBoost, LightGBM, and related ensemble models, are widely used in demand forecasting because they effectively handle non-linear relationships, mixed feature types, and noisy data (for example, different days of the week, seasons, promotions, and user habits). Recent research on food demand forecasting and supply chain management shows that gradient boosting and tree-based ensembles outperform simple linear regression in predicting future orders and required stock, reducing overstock and waste.
In inventory management, machine learning models are used to predict stock-outs, recommend reorder points, and dynamically change safety stock. Supervised approaches can use historical purchase histories, consumption rates, and contextual factors to forecast when certain items will run out or risk expiration. IBM's AI-driven inventory management idea, for example, focuses on predictive models that continuously update projections as new data comes, allowing for automated replenishment decisions rather than fixed norms. Similar supervised learning algorithms, such as gradient boosting or random forests, can be trained on user purchase histories and consumption habits to suggest what things to buy next and in what quantities, decreasing both shortages and waste.
2.4.3 Algorithms for Image and Barcode-Based Recognition (Optional Extension)
Although this research does not focus on comprehensive computer vision, several comparable systems employ picture recognition to identify food or monitor waste. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are the standard architecture for image-based tasks, and commercial systems like Winnow and similar tools in commercial kitchens use CNNs to detect plate waste and classify discarded food types from camera images, resulting in significant waste reductions (e.g., 30-50%) in hospitality environments (Djekic et al., 2025). While barcode and receipt scanning are simpler and can be handled with standard OCR and parsing techniques, future work could include lightweight CNN-based classifiers or pre-trained vision models to automate the recognition of unpackaged items (such as fruits and vegetables) in the home.
2.4.4 Natural Language Processing for Conversational Interfaces
Natural language understanding methods are critical for allowing users to interact with the system via inquiries and orders such "What can I cook with chicken and spinach that expires this week?" Traditional approaches rely on pipelines of intent classification and entity recognition: text classification models (e.g., logistic regression, support vector machines, or shallow neural networks) identify the user's intent (e.g., "request_recipe", "check_expiry"), while sequence labeling models or rule-based extractors identify ingredients and time-related entities (Abbas et al., 2022). To capture context and handle more diverse utterances, more modern systems employ deep learning models such as recurrent neural networks, transformers, and BERT-style encoders.
NLP has been utilized in food recommendation and diet-assistant contexts to interpret user queries, extract ingredient lists, match them with recipe databases, and modify results based on preferences or health constraints (Xu et al., 2024). These methods can be used in this project to create a conversational interface in which the algorithm first classifies intent before passing structured parameters (ingredients, expiry dates, dietary restrictions) to the recommendation engine. As the system collects more conversational data, it can improve its ability to understand domain-specific terms and preferences.
2.4.5 Implementation Approaches
Previous research and commercial systems addressing AI kitchen assistants, recipe recommenders, and food waste trackers have used a variety of implementation methodologies, ranging from cloud-based hybrid models to edge-deployed lightweight engines. These approaches differ in architecture, data processing, and integration levels, with trade-offs in scalability, user privacy, and real-time performance that influence the project's design decisions (Yap et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024). Common patterns include dual-mode recommendation engines, API-dependent pipelines, and sensor-augmented prototypes, which are frequently evaluated based on metrics like prediction accuracy, latency and waste reduction.
Cloud-Based Hybrid Recommendation Pipelines
Many academic and industrial solutions use cloud infrastructure for heavy computation, mixing backend ML models and frontend apps. For example, Xu et al. (2024) used a hybrid pipeline to create a personalized meal recommender: a cloud-hosted filtering stage evaluates dietary limitations and user profiles using knowledge graphs, followed by preference ranking using collaborative filtering. Similarly, Yap et al. (2024) used a KNN-SVD hybrid on scalable cloud servers to index recipe databases and perform rapid similarity searches. Access to large datasets for training, automated scalability during high demand, and quick model upgrades without reinstalling the app are all advantages. Weaknesses include latency from network calls (2-5 seconds per query), privacy risks from sending pantry data to servers, and reliance on internet connectivity, which interrupts offline meal planning.(Data Template, 2024)
Pantry Cook's case study shows this: their team replaced unreliable external recipe APIs with a proprietary cloud engine indexing 30,000+ recipes, allowing for real-time suggestions but still requiring online connection for full functioning (Data Template, 2024). Another kitchen assistant, Ladle AI, employed cloud-based transformers to generate and validate recipes while also managing dietary guidelines and appliance adjustments for vegan/gluten-free demands (Tezeract, 2025). These excel at customisation but struggle in homes with patchy Wi-Fi.
Edge and Offline-Capable Engines
To overcome cloud downsides, certain systems prioritize on-device processing. Pantry Cook has evolved to a layered fallback strategy, with primary cloud queries and offline cached recommendations as backups, lowering latency to less than 1 second and ensuring stability (Data Template, 2024). Smart kitchen prototypes such as KitchenMind use edge AI on Raspberry Pi or mobile devices, using lightweight CNNs for ingredient detection via phone cameras and local vector databases for recipe matching (Heveus, 2024). Low latency, privacy (data remains local), and offline resilience are key features for cooking settings. Model complexity is limited by mobile hardware limits, smaller datasets for customisation, and higher development expenses for cross-platform optimization (Onix Systems, 2025).
Sensor-Integrated and Conversational Implementations
Commercial trash trackers like Winnow use hardware-software hybrids: edge sensors weigh/scan discards and transmit data to cloud ML for waste classification using CNNs, resulting in 30-50% reductions in pilots (Djekic et al., 2025). Conversational agents in Ladle use on-device NLP to identify intent (e.g., BERT-lite) before cloud validation, creating recipes in 30 seconds with 70% coherence accuracy (Tezeract, 2025). Strengths include proactive features such as expiry nudges, while disadvantages include hardware costs and scalability concerns for consumer applications.

2.5 State of the Art in the Field
2.5.1 Recent Advances
In 2025, the field of AI-driven food waste reduction experienced fast progress, with innovations in predictive analytics, computer vision, and consumer-facing smart assistants bringing domestic applications closer to widespread acceptance. These developments build directly on the previously stated hybrid recommendation and inventory theories, combining real-time data from sensors, edge AI, and large language models (LLMs) to construct proactive systems that reduce waste at home. Key improvements prioritize supply chain efficiency, dynamic pricing, and tailored kitchen equipment, resulting in up to 87% waste reduction in pilots while addressing scalability for ordinary users.
Predictive Demand Forecasting and Dynamic Inventory
Machine learning models such as sophisticated gradient boosting (e.g., XGBoost variations) and transformer-based predictors now analyze multimodal data—sales history, weather, social trends, and even fridge sensor inputs—to estimate household demand with MAPE less than 10%, outperforming classical regression. In 2025, retail trials employ dynamic pricing as follows: AI reduces costs on near-expiry commodities in real time, moving 30-50% of produce from landfills to consumers. Household extensions, such as Samsung and LG's smart fridge integrations, use edge ML to track expiry via RFID tags and cameras, recommending recipes before they spoil, early experiments indicate 25% waste reduction without operator input.
Computer Vision and Waste Monitoring
Orbisk and Winnow have enhanced CNN-based kitchen systems, employing overhead cameras to classify thrown food in real time with 95% accuracy, resulting in a 70% reduction in restaurant waste by detecting trends such as over-trimming. Household adaptations developed in 2025: small "waste cams" (e.g., Zest AI) track discards via phone mounts, feeding data to cloud LLMs for targeted nudges—"use that chicken before it goes bad"—with Nestlé's trial saving 700 tonnes (1.5 million meals) and cutting 1,400 tonnes CO2. These beat conventional rule-based trackers by learning user habits and providing predicted notifications.
Generative AI and Conversational Kitchen Assistants
Next-generation applications are powered by LLMs such as fine-tuned GPT-4o and Llama versions. In beta, Ladle AI (2025) develops personalized recipes from voice queries and validates them against pantry scans and nutrition APIs in less than 2 seconds, with 80% user satisfaction. Pantry Cook's open-source engine indexes over 50k recipes using vector embeddings (e.g., Sentence Transformers), allowing for semantic searches such as "quick vegan with expiring veggies". Hybrid edge-cloud configurations provide offline fallback by combining local NLP for intent detection with cloud reranking—moving beyond 2024's basic keyword matching.
Circular Economy and Multimodal Frameworks
Emerging frameworks incorporate AI across the supply chain: computer vision sorts "ugly" fruit for secondary markets, and black army fly farms exploit ML-optimized waste streams for protein. Open-source platforms, such as Hugging Face's food-waste models, democratize access by enabling federated learning and privacy-preserving home data exchange. These 2025 SOTA systems save 40-70% on waste but remain compartmentalized; your solution advances the industry by combining forecasting, vision-optional tracking, generative NLP, and hybrid recommendations in a single mobile app.
2.5.2 Trends and Future Directions
AI research and development for food waste reduction and smart kitchens is developing swiftly, with numerous clear themes shaping what future systems will look like. These trends extend beyond basic recipe apps to highly intelligent, context-aware assistants that can adapt to specific households, devices, and even larger sustainability goals.
Shift toward Proactive & Personalized Assistants
A noteworthy trend is the shift from reactive tools (when the user requests a recipe or reports waste) to proactive, always-on assistants. Instead than awaiting the user's interaction, future systems will:
Monitor pantry and fridge changes continuously (via purchase history, barcode scans, or optional sensors).
Predict when certain items are likely to expire or be forgotten.
Proactively suggest actions, such as: “You have tomatoes and spinach expiring in two days; here are three quick dinner ideas.”
This change is strongly dependent on enhanced machine learning models for preference learning, time-series prediction for consumption patterns, and better user modeling. It is perfectly aligned with your planned app: the assistant will actively aid the user in planning meals and shopping to avoid waste before it occurs, rather than simply "respond" with recipes.
Edge and On-Device Intelligence
Another important trend is to move more AI processing to the user's device (edge computing), rather than depending solely on cloud servers. There are a number of causes behind this:
Privacy: Food purchases, dietary preferences and daily routines are sensitive. Keeping most data and models on the device reduces privacy concerns.
Latency: On-device models can respond in under a second, which is critical in a kitchen context where users want immediate suggestions.
Offline use: Not all households have stable internet, meaning that offline capable recommendation and tracking are important for reliability.
The project suggests a hybrid architecture: lightweight models for core tasks (intent recognition, simple preference ranking, basic expiry logic) run on the phone, while heavier training and periodic updates can happen in the cloud. Over time, frameworks like TensorFlow Lite, ONNX Runtime Mobile and similar tools will make it easier to deploy compressed models on consumer devices, making the AI assistant faster and more trustworthy.
Multimodal and Context-Aware AI
Future systems will gradually mix many data types—text, photos, barcodes, sensor readings, and calendar events—to gain a more complete picture of the user's kitchen and life. This multimodal trend indicates:
Using the camera to recognize ingredients or leftovers.
Combining calendar data (busy days, events) with meal planning: simpler recipes on busy evenings, batch cooking before a heavy week.
Considering environmental context: local weather (e.g., more salads in hot weather), seasonal availability, and local store offers.
This is made possible by advancements in multimodal models, which can handle text, pictures, and structured data simultaneously. For your assistant, this means a future roadmap in which, in addition to simple text inquiries, the user can point a camera at the fridge and ask, "What can I make tonight?" and the system will respond based on visual recognition, expiry dates, and personal preferences.
Integration with Smart Appliances and IoT
There is a clear trend toward deeper integration with smart home devices and kitchen appliances:
Smart fridges that track contents via built-in cameras or weight sensors.
Smart ovens that can auto-set temperature and time based on the selected recipe.
Smart scales or bins that measure the amount and type of food being discarded.
These devices, when paired with AI, can automate certain aspects of inventory tracking and waste measurement, decreasing human work. For your project, this suggests creating an API-friendly architecture that can later connect to such devices. Even if your initial implementation is limited to mobile devices, keeping the design flexible will enable future integrations with fridges, voice assistants, or smart bins easier.
Stronger Focus on Sustainability Metrics and Behavior Change
Another clear direction is the focus on quantifiable sustainability metrics and behavior change, not just technical performance:
Apps increasingly show users their “impact dashboard”: kilograms of waste avoided, CO2 emissions saved and money saved over time.
Systems are using behavioral science (nudges, gamification, social comparison) to keep users engaged and encourage long-term habit change.
Studies are moving from short lab experiments to longer field trials, measuring sustained waste reduction over months rather than weeks.
For your project, success depends not just on model correctness, but also on how well the assistant influences user behavior. Include features like weekly summaries ("This week you saved X kg of food and Y MYR") and tiny targets ("Use all fresh vegetables before Sunday") to match with this trend and boost your rating afterward.
Emerging Technologies: Quantum, Federated Learning, and Beyond
Some emerging technologies are still in the early stages but are worth mentioning as future directions:
Quantum computing: In the long run, quantum optimization and quantum-enhanced machine learning could aid in the solution of difficult resource allocation problems, such as creating optimal meal plans and shopping lists under several constraints. This is not now viable for consumer apps, but it hints at speedier, more general optimizations in the future.
Federated learning and privacy-preserving AI: Instead of providing raw user data to central servers, models can be trained on devices and only communicate aggregated results. This enables learning from a large number of users (for example, common waste patterns, popular "save it before expiry" recipes) while protecting individual data. This is especially relevant for a food-waste assistant, where privacy is essential yet learning from community patterns is beneficial.
Explainable and trustworthy AI: There is a trend toward making recommendations interpretable, such as "This recipe is recommended because it uses your chicken expiring tomorrow and matches your usual low-sodium preference." This increases user trust in the system and helps them comprehend trade-offs, particularly when it comes to balancing health, costs, and waste reduction.
How These Trends Shape The Project
Taken together, these trends suggest that the “state of the art” is moving toward:
Proactive and personalized assistants rather than static apps.
Hybrid edge–cloud architectures for speed, privacy, and resilience.
Multimodal, context-aware intelligence that understands ingredients, time, and lifestyle.
Deep integration with smart home devices and sustainability dashboards.
Privacy-preserving and explainable AI techniques to build trust.
The proposed AI kitchen assistant aligns with these goals: it focuses on proactive, hybrid recommendations; it utilizes AI to predict grocery needs; it intends to integrate expiry tracking and inventory; and it can evolve to include multimodal input and smart appliance integration.
2.6 Critical Review of Similar Products and Systems
Several existing products and research prototypes aim to help users manage food at home or reduce food waste, but they typically address only parts of the problem. These systems can be grouped into three main categories: consumer recipe and pantry apps, community and surplus-sharing platforms, and AI-driven monitoring systems used mainly in commercial kitchens. Reviewing these solutions highlights their strengths and limitations, and clarifies how the proposed AI kitchen assistant builds on and extends current work.
2.6.1 Review of Similar Projects
Consumer-focused apps such as SuperCook, Kitche, Nosh, and NoWaste mainly target recipe discovery and basic household inventory management. SuperCook allows users to input ingredients manually or via voice and then suggests recipes that match those items, supporting over 2,000 ingredients and simple filters like cuisine or diet type (Apple, 2024). This helps users “cook from what they have” but does not track expiry dates or learn from past behavior, so the user must repeatedly update their pantry records. Kitche and Nosh move slightly further toward automation by using receipt or barcode scanning to build inventory and, in Nosh’s case, by analyzing usage patterns to generate suggested shopping lists (Shapiro, 2024; Postharvest Technologies, 2024). However, their recipe integration and expiry prioritization remain limited, and they still rely heavily on user discipline to keep data accurate over time. NoWaste focuses on lists and expiry reminders, promoting first-in-first-out use of ingredients, but offers no recipe recommendations or predictive functions (Shapiro, 2024).
Community and surplus-sharing apps such as Too Good To Go and OLIO address food waste at the retail and neighborhood level rather than at the level of personal kitchen management. Too Good To Go connects users with restaurants and supermarkets that offer discounted “surprise bags” of unsold food, helping divert surplus that would otherwise be discarded (Food Tank, 2021). OLIO enables individuals to share excess food with nearby users, creating a peer-to-peer redistribution network. Both are impactful for reducing external waste and saving money, but they do not help users track what is in their own fridges, plan meals around expiring items, or avoid overbuying in the first place.
Academic and commercial AI systems such as Winnow and Kitro focus on monitoring and reducing food waste in professional kitchens, using computer vision and sensors to weigh and classify discarded food. Studies report that these systems can reduce waste by 30–50% by providing detailed analytics and feedback to staff (Djekic et al., 2025). However, they are designed for restaurants and institutions, requiring specialized hardware, installation, and training. Household-oriented digital food tools reviewed by Kwasny et al. (2025) and Abbas et al. (2022) often emphasize healthy eating or sustainability messaging, but only a small fraction combine real-time inventory, personalized recommendations, and behavior change strategies in a coherent way. Trials of smartphone apps for food waste show that while users can reduce waste and adopt healthier habits, engagement tends to decline when apps require high manual effort and lack deep personalization (Mathisen et al., 2022).
2.6.2 How the Proposed Project Differs
The proposed AI kitchen assistant builds on these existing systems but differs in several important ways. First, it targets the household as the primary setting, combining features that are currently split across multiple apps: inventory tracking, expiry management, recipe recommendation, and grocery planning. Unlike SuperCook or NoWaste, which either focus on recipes or on static lists, the proposed system is explicitly expiry-aware, meaning that suggested meals will prioritize ingredients that are close to expiring rather than simply matching any available items. Second, the assistant is designed to learn from user behavior—purchase history, cooking patterns, and typical serving sizes—to predict what groceries the user is likely to need next, rather than relying solely on manually created shopping lists.
Third, the project aims to provide a natural language interface that allows users to ask questions such as “What can I cook with my chicken and spinach that are expiring this week?” and receive tailored recommendations. Existing apps typically rely on forms or basic keyword search; a conversational approach lowers the barrier to regular use, especially for busy users. Finally, the proposed system adopts a unified, AI-driven architecture that combines hybrid recommendation algorithms, predictive models for grocery needs, and user-centered design principles. This contrasts with many current solutions, which either use simple rule-based logic for reminders or deploy advanced AI only in commercial settings.

3.0 Methodology
3.1. Introduction
This chapter outlines a detailed and methodical approach to developing the AI Smart Kitchen Assistant mobile application and supporting web platform. Building directly on Chapter 2's literature review, which identified critical flaws in existing food waste management systems such as fragmented functionality, excessive manual data entry requirements, a lack of explicit expiry date prioritization in recommendations and insufficient integration of predictive grocery planning, this section translates theoretical research gaps into a practical and executable implementation strategy.
The chosen development technique leverages a hybrid methodology that combines Design Thinking for comprehensive user-centered requirements elicitation with Agile development, which uses 2-week sprints for iterative prototyping and refinement. This combination ensures that development is firmly rooted in real-world household user needs while also providing the flexibility needed to iteratively improve complex AI components like hybrid recommendation algorithms (KNN-SVD), time-series forecasting models, and natural language processing for conversational interfaces. The technique focuses on three main areas: the entire software development process (Section 3.2), thorough user research and requirements gathering methodologies (Section 3.3), and high-level system design using UML modeling (Section 3.4).
This organized yet adaptable framework has been specifically designed to meet the practical restrictions of a final-year project, such as limited development time (20 weeks each semester), solitary developer resources, and the necessity for regular supervisor demos. The approach ensures delivery of a functional minimal viable product (MVP) by Week 19, with comprehensive evaluation and documentation completed by Week 20, while adhering to user-centered design principles and industry best practices identified in the literature review.
3.2 Software Development Methodology
3.2.1. Chosen Methodology and Justification
Following a systematic evaluation of established software development methodologies, including the Waterfall model (excessively rigid for AI prototyping and requirement evolution), the Spiral model (overly formal risk analysis processes unsuitable for individual development), and pure Scrum (team-oriented ceremonies impractical for solo work), this project employs a hybrid Design Thinking + Agile development methodology based on 2-week sprints.
Design Thinking, as defined by IDEO (2015), is a structured five-stage human-centered process (Empathise, Define, Ideate, Prototype, and Test) that assures development begins with a thorough grasp of target consumers' food waste pain areas. This methodology directly operationalizes the user-centered design (UCD) and persuasive design principles identified as critical success factors in the literature review (Nkwo et al., 2021; Mathisen et al., 2022), preventing the development of features that do not address actual household behaviors such as forgetting about perishable vegetables or overbuying staple ingredients.
Agile sprints (8 x 2-week cycles spanning Weeks 4-19) then allow for rapid iterative development using the usual pattern of sprint planning, daily development, user testing, sprint review, and retrospective. This hybrid methodology provides numerous significant advantages that are specifically tailored to this project's technical and temporal constraints:
User requirement validation: The empathy phase of Design Thinking uses mixed-methods research (50 surveys, 10 interviews) to empirically validate feature priorities, assuring good development ROI by focusing on validated needs rather than assumptions.
Technical risk mitigation: Early MVP delivery of core functionality (inventory CRUD + expiry notifications by Sprint 2) allows for thorough testing of additional AI layers (recommendation engines, forecasting models) before excessive resource commitment.
Timeline alignment: Bi-weekly sprint cycles generate tangible deliverables that are aligned with FYP goals, allowing for regular supervisor progress demonstrations and timely course adjustments.
AI/ML development suitability: Iterative sprints allow for rigorous model experimentation, hyperparameter tuning, and A/B testing (e.g., hybrid vs. content-based recommendations) with real user interaction data.
Solo developer optimisation: With little ceremony overhead (15-minute daily standups, bi-weekly demos), coding time is maximized while retaining development discipline and progress visibility.
Academic and industry relevance: This exact hybrid method reflects current best practice at prominent technological businesses (Google, Airbnb, IDEO), while also meeting academic criteria for rationalized, systematic development processes with demonstrable outputs.
Alternative approaches were rejected for particular reasons: Waterfall lacks iteration for AI enhancement, Spiral requires unnecessary formal documentation and Pure Agile risks scope creep without Design Thinking's user validation base.

Total Questions: 16 structured items comprising demographics (Q1-4), food waste pain point severity assessment via 5-point Likert scales (Q5-9), feature priority multi-selection (Q10), current tracking behaviours (Q11-12), input method preferences (Q13), and two open-ended qualitative probes (Q14-15)

Use Case Diagram
The Use Case Diagram depicts the functional interactions of users with the AI Smart Kitchen Assistant system. It presents a high-level overview of how various players use the system's capabilities to efficiently reduce household food waste.
The primary actor in the system is the User, who represents household members who use the mobile application. Users can register and log onto the system, manage their personal profiles and preferences, and keep track of the ingredients they own. The ingredient management tools allow users to add, amend, and view food products as well as their expiration dates. The system regularly monitors these expiry dates and delivers notification alerts when things are about to expire, encouraging timely use and decreasing waste.
Users can also interact with intelligent features that are powered by AI. The algorithm recommends recipes based on available components, expiration dates, and user preferences. Users can also interact with an AI chatbot to ask inquiries about cooking, expiring ingredients, and meal suggestions. Furthermore, the system provides predictive grocery suggestions based on previous purchase history and consumption habits, assisting consumers in making more informed shopping decisions.
The Administrator is a supporting actor who is in charge of keeping the system running. Administrative activities include managing the recipe database, updating system content, and monitoring system data to ensure proper operation and suggestions.
Overall, the Use Case Diagram specifies the primary system features and clearly depicts how users and administrators interact with the AI Smart Kitchen Assistant to achieve the goal of minimizing home food waste.
Package Diagram
The Package Diagram depicts the AI Smart Kitchen Assistant system's high-level modular structure and the relationships between its primary components. The system is organized into logical packages to increase maintainability, scalability, and accountability.
The Mobile Application (UI) package acts as the primary interface between the user and the system. The User Management package handles login and personalization capabilities, while the Inventory Management package allows users to manage food products and expiration dates. The UI also relies on the AI & Recommendation Engine to provide intelligent cooking suggestions, grocery forecasts, and chatbot responses. Furthermore, it relies on the Notification System to display alerts about expired ingredients.
The Database package is used by the User Management, Inventory Management, and AI & Recommendation Engine packages to store and retrieve persistent data such as user profiles, ingredient data, recipes, and usage history. The Notification System uses the Inventory Management package to determine when expiry-based warnings should be triggered.
All relationships in the diagram are represented by dependency relationships, which are depicted as dashed arrows and indicate that one package requires the services or data of another package to function properly. This modular form promotes separation of concerns and is consistent with the system scope outlined in the project proposal.
Class Diagram
The Class Diagram depicts the static structure of the AI Smart Kitchen Assistant system, including its core classes, properties, methods, and their relationships. The diagram is based on the system's package architecture to guarantee that logical modules are consistent with object-oriented design.
The User class, which represents a household user of the mobile application, is fundamental to the system. The User class stores personal information and dietary preferences and is linked to the AuthService class, which handles authentication and secure access to the system. The MobileAppUI class works with the User class to provide an interface for logging in, managing ingredients, and accessing intelligent features.
Food items are represented by the Ingredient class, which contains critical information such as quantity, category, and expiration date. The InventoryManager class handles a collection of Ingredient objects and has a composition connection with them, implying that ingredients exist exclusively in the context of an inventory. The User class has an aggregation relationship with the InventoryManager, indicating that each user owns an inventory, which is managed independently by the system.
The RecommendationEngine, Recipe, and Chatbot classes handle intelligent features. The RecommendationEngine works with the Recipe class to generate appropriate recipe recommendations, and it relies on Ingredient data to make reliable choices. The Chatbot class handles user inquiries and relies on the RecommendationEngine to return context-aware responses, allowing for natural language interaction within the system.
Notification functionality is represented by the NotificationManager and Notification classes. The NotificationManager has a composition relationship with Notification objects since notifications are created and managed solely by the system. The InventoryManager is responsible for retrieving expiry-related information, which allows the system to send out warnings when food products are about to expire.
The Database class represents persistent data storage, and the InventoryManager, RecommendationEngine, and NotificationManager use dependency relationships to access it. This architecture maintains separation of concerns by eliminating direct interaction between the user interface and the database layer.
To enforce encapsulation, all class attributes are defined as private, whereas methods are public, allowing for controlled interaction between system components. Overall, the Class Diagram shows a modular, scalable, and maintainable system design that meets the functional criteria and objectives mentioned in the project proposal.

Activity Diagram
The activity diagram depicts the whole workflow of the AI Smart Kitchen Assistant system and how activities are divided among three key parties: the User, the System, and the AI Engine. Swimlanes are used to clearly clarify each party's responsibilities and increase the readability of the process flow.
When the user first launches the mobile application, the procedure begins in the User swimlane. The user is then prompted to log in or create an account. A decision point determines whether the user is a new or existing user. New users must register their accounts, whereas existing users can log in instantly. After successful authentication, the user can modify their profile and personal preferences before adding or updating kitchen ingredients.

Following the recipe recommendation process, the AI engine makes grocery suggestions based on the user's past ingredient consumption. The first objective of this project was to explore and identify effective methods of reducing household food waste through ingredient tracking, recipe suggestions, and reminder-based support. The survey findings show that the target users are mostly young adults and home users who cook at different levels of frequency. Out of 63 respondents, 28 respondents were between 18 and 24 years old, representing 44.4% of the sample. Another 22 respondents, or 34.9%, were between 25 and 34 years old. 4.3 Findings of Objective 3: Development and Implementation of EcoBite
The third objective was to develop and implement the mobile app and website by integrating the planned features into a working prototype. The final product consists of the EcoBite Flutter mobile application and a promotional website hosted through GitHub Pages.
4.3.1 Mobile Application Development
EcoBite was developed using Dart programming language with the Flutter framework. Flutter was chosen because it supports cross-platform mobile development, allowing the same codebase to be used for Android and iOS development. During implementation, the Android version was tested directly on a physical Android device.
The mobile app includes a structured navigation system with six main areas: Home, Items, Recipes, AI, Shop and Profile. This navigation makes it easier for users to move between the main functions without needing to search through many menus.
The authentication feature was implemented using Firebase Authentication. When users open the app, they are asked to log in or create an account. This allows the system to store user-related kitchen data separately and supports a more personalized experience.
Figure 4.3.1: Firebase Login Screen
Firestore was used as the database for storing user inventory items and preference-related information. This allows kitchen items, expiry dates, quantities, and user settings to be saved and retrieved when needed. The database connection supports the app’s goal of maintaining a persistent kitchen inventory.
4.3.2 Kitchen Inventory and Expiry Tracking
The kitchen inventory feature allows users to add ingredients or food items with details such as item name, quantity, category and expiry date. Users can edit or delete items after they are added. The system also includes filters for viewing all items, urgent items and expired items.
Expiry tracking was implemented using date comparison logic. Items expiring within one day are shown with a stronger warning colour, while items expiring within two to three days are shown with a light yellow background. Expired items are marked with a clear expired indicator. This result supports the project objective of increasing user awareness of food items that should be used first.
The barcode scanner feature was added to make item entry more convenient. Instead of typing all details manually, users can scan a product barcode and use available product information to add the item into the inventory. This improves usability because manual data entry can be time-consuming.
Figure 4.3.2: Barcode Scanner Screen
4.3.3 Recipe Suggestion Development
The recipe suggestion feature was developed to recommend meals based on items available in the kitchen inventory. The system compares recipe ingredients with the user’s inventory and ranks recipes based on ingredient availability. Recipes with more matching ingredients are given higher priority.
The feature also considers dietary preferences. For example, if users select a preference such as halal, recipes that are not suitable should be filtered out. This makes the recommendation more personalized and prevents unsuitable recipe suggestions from appearing.
Recipe cards also show which ingredients are available and which ingredients are missing. Users can add missing ingredients into the grocery plan. This creates a connection between the recipe suggestion feature and the grocery planning feature.
4.3.4 Predictive Grocery Plan Development
The predictive grocery plan feature was developed to help users identify what they need to buy. The grocery plan uses missing recipe ingredients, basic staples, and user preferences to generate suggestions. This feature helps users avoid buying duplicate items because the suggestions are connected to their current inventory.
The grocery screen includes a checklist system so users can mark planned items while shopping. Users can also add custom grocery items and transfer bought items into the inventory. This supports the project goal of improving grocery planning and reducing unnecessary purchases.
Figure 4.3.3: Grocery Checklist and Finish Shopping Screen
4.3.5 AI Assistant Development
The AI assistant feature was developed to provide food-related support inside the app. The assistant uses a structured knowledge base to respond to questions related to food waste, recipes, storage, grocery planning, and kitchen inventory. The assistant was designed to stay focused on the EcoBite domain rather than answering unrelated questions.
To improve the user experience, a thinking animation and gradual response display were added. This makes the assistant feel more natural and avoids responses appearing too suddenly. Although the assistant is not a full large-scale AI model like ChatGPT, it functions as a focused helper for food management and waste reduction.
4.3.6 Notification and Preference Development
Notification support was added so that users can receive reminders before food expires. Reminder settings can be adjusted in the profile page. The profile page also includes account settings, dietary preferences, cooking preferences, household size, and dark mode. These preferences improve personalization and allow the app to better match user needs.
Figure 4.3.4: Dark Mode Interface Example
4.3.7 Promotional Website Development
The promotional website was developed using HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. The purpose of the website is to introduce EcoBite, explain its features, show app screenshots, answer frequently asked questions, and provide a download button for the Android APK.
The website follows the same visual identity as the mobile app, using white, light green, and dark green colours. It includes smooth animations, feature preview buttons, an app screenshot display, a FAQ section, and download buttons linked to the GitHub Release APK file. Cooking frequency varied among respondents. The largest group, 20 respondents or 31.7%, cooked once a week. Another 17 respondents, or 27.0%, cooked a few times a week. 5.2 Conclusion
In conclusion, the EcoBite project successfully achieved its main aim of developing a smart kitchen assistant application to support household food waste reduction. The final prototype provides users with tools to monitor kitchen inventory, track expiry dates, receive recipe suggestions, plan groceries, scan barcodes, ask food-related questions, and customize their preferences. Instead of only reminding users about expiry dates, EcoBite combines several related functions into one system. For example, an item added to the kitchen inventory can influence expiry reminders, recipe suggestions, and grocery planning. This makes the app more practical because the features support one another rather than working separately.
The survey results also support the conclusion that EcoBite is useful and understandable for users. Most respondents found the app easy to understand, and many agreed that the expiry indicators, grocery plan, and recipe suggestions were helpful. The positive response toward predictive grocery planning and waste-saving tips shows that users value features that help them make decisions faster and reduce unnecessary waste.

Some users suggested clearer expiry alerts, more recipe variety, faster loading times, better barcode scanning, more customizable notifications, larger font options, and more language support. 5.2.1 Achievement of Project Objectives
The project objectives were achieved through the design, development, and evaluation of the EcoBite system. The first objective was achieved by identifying user needs related to food expiry, grocery planning, and meal decision-making. The second objective was achieved by designing the app and website around these needs. The third objective was achieved by developing the functional mobile app and promotional website.
The implementation of the kitchen inventory and expiry tracking features directly addressed the problem of forgotten food items. The recipe suggestion feature helped users make use of available ingredients. The predictive grocery plan helped users identify what they need to buy instead of purchasing unnecessary items. The AI assistant provided additional food-related guidance, while the barcode scanner improved convenience when adding items.
From the evaluation results, users responded positively to the app’s purpose and features. This shows that the final output is aligned with the project aim and has potential for real-world use if further enhanced.
5.2.2 Limitations of the Study
Although the project achieved its objectives, several limitations were identified during development and evaluation. Firstly, the application was mainly tested on Android. Since iOS requires Apple development tools, certificates, and TestFlight or App Store distribution, the iOS version was not fully tested in this project phase. Therefore, the current downloadable APK is only suitable for Android users.
Secondly, the recipe database is limited to the recipes included during development. Although the app contains a variety of recipes and website references, it does not yet have a large-scale dynamic recipe database like commercial recipe platforms. As a result, recipe variety may still be limited for users with very specific ingredients or dietary needs.
Thirdly, the AI assistant is a focused food-related assistant rather than a full advanced AI model. It can answer questions related to food waste, storage, recipes, and grocery planning based on the app’s knowledge base, but it may not handle very complex or unrelated questions.
Fourthly, barcode scanning depends on available product data. If an item barcode is not found in the database or lookup source, the user may still need to enter details manually. This means barcode scanning improves convenience but does not completely remove manual input.
Fifthly, the survey results were based on 63 respondents, which is useful for FYP evaluation but still limited compared to a larger real-world user study. The respondents mainly represented young adults, so future evaluation should include a wider group of users such as families, working parents, and older adults.
Finally, the app update process is not fully automatic. The website can provide an APK download link, but Android users still need to manually confirm installation when updating the app. For iOS, updates would need to be managed through TestFlight or the App Store.
5.2.3 Project Contribution
This project contributes by providing a mobile application that combines several food management features in one system. Many existing apps focus only on recipes, grocery lists, or reminders separately. EcoBite attempts to connect these functions through the user’s kitchen inventory so that the app can provide more relevant support.
The project also contributes to sustainable technology by encouraging users to reduce food waste through practical daily actions. Instead of presenting sustainability only as information, EcoBite turns it into app functions such as expiry indicators, waste-saving tips, recipe suggestions, and grocery planning. This makes the sustainability goal more actionable for users.
From a development perspective, the project demonstrates the use of Flutter, Firebase, Firestore, notification services, barcode scanning, and web promotion in a single FYP system. The promotional website also contributes by making the app easier to explain and distribute to users.
From a user experience perspective, EcoBite shows how a simple visual design, clear navigation, and connected features can make kitchen management easier. The survey results suggest that users responded positively to the app’s core concept and found its main features useful.
5.3 Recommendations and Future Enhancements
Based on the findings and limitations, several recommendations are suggested for future development.
Firstly, the expiry reminder system should be improved. Some respondents suggested clearer expiry date alerts. Future versions can include stronger notification options, reminder frequency settings, quiet hours, and priority reminders for items expiring within one day. This would make the reminder system more flexible and useful for different user habits.
Secondly, the recipe database should be expanded. Users suggested more recipe variety and better recipe search. Future work can include a larger recipe database with more cuisines, cooking times, dietary filters, and ingredient substitutions. This would make the recipe suggestion feature more helpful, especially for users with limited ingredients or specific food preferences.
Thirdly, the barcode scanner can be improved by connecting it to a larger product database. This would increase the chance of recognizing scanned products and reduce the need for manual entry. Future versions can also allow users to contribute missing barcode information to improve the database over time.
Fourthly, the AI assistant can be enhanced with more advanced natural language processing. The current assistant is useful as a focused helper, but future versions could connect to a stronger AI model or cloud-based AI service. This would allow the assistant to provide more detailed answers, better recipe guidance, and more natural conversation.
Fifthly, the app can include a meal planning calendar. Some users suggested adding a meal planning feature. A weekly calendar would allow users to plan breakfast, lunch, and dinner using items in their inventory. This would strengthen the connection between recipe suggestions and grocery planning.
Sixthly, the app can include food waste progress tracking. For example, users could see how many items they used before expiry, how many expired, and how much money they may have saved. This would make the impact of using EcoBite more visible and could motivate users to continue using the app.
Seventhly, accessibility should be improved. Some respondents suggested larger font options and better usability. Future versions can include adjustable font sizes, clearer contrast settings, screen reader support, and simplified onboarding screens for first-time users.
Eighthly, the app should be tested with a wider range of users. Future evaluation should include students, families, working adults, and users who cook frequently. A longer testing period would also help measure whether EcoBite actually changes user behaviour over time.
Ninthly, the app update process can be improved. Since the APK download is hosted through GitHub Releases, the app can include a version check feature using Firebase. When a new version is available, the app can notify users and open the download link. This would make updates easier for Android users.
Finally, iOS deployment should be considered in future work. Since Flutter supports cross-platform development, the app can be prepared for iOS testing through TestFlight. This would allow EcoBite to reach a wider group of users beyond Android.
